This workflow starts with a measured axis at each site. Its azimuth is its bearing clockwise from north. The horizon altitude is the vertical angle to the skyline in that direction. Together with the site latitude these determine declination, the angular distance north or south of the celestial equator. We first calculate it from field measurements, then investigate one Maltese doorway and finally compare a group of entrances.

For help with any package function and its parameters, type ? followed by its name in the R console, for example ?az2dec.

1. Data input

Import your observations

Give each site one row in your spreadsheet. Record one compass bearing along the axis, looking outwards, and the horizon altitude in the same direction. For the two illustrative sites used here, the worksheet is called Compass. Replace the path below with the path on your computer.

library(skyscapeR)
survey <- readxl::read_excel("/Users/yourname/Research/field-survey.xlsx", sheet = "Compass")
survey
#> # A tibble: 2 × 7
#>   id     survey_date         latitude longitude elevation_m mag_az_deg
#>   <chr>  <dttm>                 <dbl>     <dbl>       <dbl>      <dbl>
#> 1 Site A 2026-08-28 00:00:00     51.2     -1.83         105       84.5
#> 2 Site B 2026-08-28 00:00:00     51.2     -1.77         126       96.5
#> # ℹ 1 more variable: horizon_alt_deg <dbl>

Use id for the site name and survey_date for the date of the observation. Enter latitude and longitude in decimal degrees (south and west negative), site elevation in metres, and bearing and horizon altitude in degrees. Keep the measured bearing in its original column.

Reduce compass readings to true azimuth

The package uses the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF-14), evaluated by oce for the site coordinates and survey date. Magnetic north changes with place and time. Use the date the compass was read. reduct.compass() adds the local magnetic declination to each bearing. Passing the locations and dates in worksheet order keeps each correction with its observation.

locations <- as.matrix(survey[c("latitude", "longitude", "elevation_m")])
compass <- reduct.compass(loc = locations, mag.az = survey$mag_az_deg,
                          date = as.Date(survey$survey_date), ID = survey$id)
compass[c("ID", "Magnetic.Azimuth", "Mag.Dec", "True.Azimuth")]
#>       ID Magnetic.Azimuth   Mag.Dec True.Azimuth
#> 1 Site A             84.5 0.6373595         85.1
#> 2 Site B             96.5 0.6535282         97.2

Mag.Dec is the correction between magnetic and true north, in degrees east. For a manual reduction, calculate magnetic declination with mag.dec(locations, as.Date(survey$survey_date)), then add it to the magnetic bearing and wrap the result with %% 360. Alternatively pass an independently known correction as magdec to reduct.compass(). If your spreadsheet already records true azimuths, use that column directly in the next step.

Reduce a theodolite survey with a solar sight

For field instructions on the sun-sight method, see the survey appendix in Clive Ruggles (1999), Astronomy in Prehistoric Britain and Ireland. A theodolite’s horizontal circle can have an arbitrary zero. In this alternative to the compass workflow, the surveyor recorded the circle reading to the Sun’s right limb and the local time of that sight. reduct.theodolite() uses the predicted azimuth of that limb to orient every structural reading made with the same circle setting. ten() converts the recorded degrees, minutes and seconds to decimal degrees.

The solar sight and later celestial examples need Swiss Ephemeris files covering the observation date. For optimum use of skyscapeR, install swephRdata before starting the solar example, then restart R. If you already keep the files locally, set SE_EPHE_PATH before loading skyscapeR.

install.packages("swephRdata", repos = "https://rstub.r-universe.dev", type = "source")
library(skyscapeR)
site_location <- c(51.18, -1.83, 105)
theo <- reduct.theodolite(loc = site_location, az = c(298.40, 302.35),
                          date = "2016/02/20", time = "11:07:17",
                          tz = "Europe/London", az.sun = ten(327, 29, 50),
                          limb = "right")
theo$Altitude <- c(2, 5)  # Horizon altitudes measured along the two sightlines
theo[c("Uncorrected.Azimuth", "Sun.Az", "True.Azimuth", "Altitude")]
#>   Uncorrected.Azimuth Sun.Az True.Azimuth Altitude
#> 1              298.40 160.09     130.9928        2
#> 2              302.35 160.09     134.9428        5

Sun.Az is the calculated solar reference bearing. To calculate it separately, use sunAz().

Convert each sightline to celestial declination

Carry each true azimuth forward with the latitude of the site and the measured altitude of the horizon along that sightline. For the compass sites the altitude is in the original worksheet.

survey$true_az_deg <- compass$True.Azimuth %% 360
survey$dec_deg <- az2dec(survey$true_az_deg, loc = survey$latitude,
                         alt = survey$horizon_alt_deg)
survey[c("id", "true_az_deg", "horizon_alt_deg", "dec_deg")]
#> # A tibble: 2 × 4
#>   id     true_az_deg horizon_alt_deg dec_deg
#>   <chr>        <dbl>           <dbl>   <dbl>
#> 1 Site A        85.1             1.2     3.7
#> 2 Site B        97.2             3.4    -2

For the theodolite sightlines the reduced bearings and skyline heights are already together in theo.

theo$Declination <- az2dec(theo$True.Azimuth, loc = theo$Latitude, alt = theo$Altitude)
theo[c("True.Azimuth", "Altitude", "Declination")]
#>   True.Azimuth Altitude Declination
#> 1     130.9928        2   -22.80645
#> 2     134.9428        5   -22.05407

2. Single site analysis

Going deeper into skyscape analysis at Mnajdra South

What celestial objects could be seen through the doorway of Mnajdra South? Start with its position and the true bearings of its two edges, surveyed by Tore Lomsdalen and published by Silva and Lomsdalen (2025).

door <- malta_entrances[1, ]
door[, c("Name", "Latitude", "Longitude", "Az L", "Az R")]
#> # A tibble: 1 × 5
#>   Name          Latitude Longitude `Az L` `Az R`
#>   <chr>            <dbl>     <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 Mnajdra South     35.8      14.4   90.9   99.1
edges <- c(door[["Az L"]], door[["Az R"]])
door_frame <- c(edges[1], mean(edges), edges[2])

Importing site horizons

If you surveyed the horizon in situ, import your observations as pairs of true azimuth and angular altitude in degrees. Supply the location of the instrument, including elevation in metres. These examples illustrate alternatives using your own files.

profile <- read.csv("/Users/yourname/Research/surveyed-horizon.csv")
survey_location <- c(51.18, -1.83, 105)  # latitude, longitude, elevation in metres
surveyed <- createHor(az = profile$azimuth, alt = profile$altitude,
                      loc = survey_location)
plot(surveyed)

With a panoramic photograph, importPanorama() detects the skyline at the boundary between the transparent sky and the foreground. Use measured bearings and altitudes of identifiable landmarks to calibrate the image.

photo <- importPanorama("doorway-east.png", loc = survey_location,
                        az.lim = c(75, 115), alt.lim = c(-5, 10), calibrate = TRUE)
plot(photo, show.skyline = TRUE)

A digital terrain model offers another way to reconstruct the horizon. HeyWhatsThat can generate a profile online from the site’s coordinates. Here the observer stands 1.6 m above the ground.

hwt <- createHWT(lat = door$Latitude, lon = door$Longitude,
                 elevation = 1.6, name = "Mnajdra South")

Alternatively, export a Horizon for Stellarium ZIP from PeakFinder and read it with importPeakFinder(). A silhouettes SVG exported from the same viewpoint adds foreground ridge detail.

pf <- importPeakFinder("landscape.zip", svg = "panorama.svg")

A local elevation raster gives control over the resolution, search distance and observer height. A finer DEM can resolve ridges smoothed away in a coarser one, provided it covers the terrain out to the visible skyline. With terra and png installed, createHor() can also render the terrain panorama.

terrain <- createHor(dem = "survey-dem.tif", loc = c(door$Latitude, door$Longitude),
                     eye.height = 1.6, max.distance = 10000, image.dir = tempdir())
plot(terrain)

You can send any of these horizons to Stellarium with exportStellarium(). For Mnajdra South a horizon generated from a 5 m DEM is already available with the published data. Its row index matches the entrance table. Read the skyline heights at the doorway edges and convert them to declinations.

hor <- malta_horizons[[1]]
hor2alt(hor, edges)
#> [1] 4.40 3.45
plot(hor, xrange = c(75, 115))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

door_dec <- az2dec(edges, loc = hor)

Equinoctial sunrise

Zero declination falls within the opening. For a worked year near the middle of the phase, the spring equinox falls on 17 April 3250 BCE in the proleptic Julian calendar. Find when the Sun’s centre crosses the terrain skyline. Compare its track at the sunrise declination (solid orange) with the equinox reference (dotted blue).

year <- "3250 BC"
sun <- riseset(hor, date = "3250 BC/04/17", obj = "sun",
               calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")$rise
sun
#>          date     time azimuth declination
#> 1 -3249/04/17 05:24:42 93.0919  -0.1716493
sky <- sketchSky(hor, time = paste(sun$date, sun$time), calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")
equinox <- skyObject("Equinox", year = year, loc = hor, col = "steelblue", lty = 3)
sunrise_track <- skyObject(sun$declination, year = year, loc = hor, col = "orange")
plot(hor, sky = sky, obj = list(equinox, sunrise_track), xrange = c(85, 105))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

At local sunrise (05:25 UT), the Sun still has declination −0.172°. It reaches the equinox later that day, around 16:06 UT. At the rising bearing the equinox reference therefore passes 0.28° above the disc centre. The orange track through the centre uses the declination at sunrise. Compare the rising bearing with the two edges and the centre. These correspond to different claims about what someone standing inside the temple could observe.

The heliacal rising of the Pleiades

Ventura, Foderà Serio and Hoskin (1993), Possible Tally Stones at Mnajdra, Malta, proposed the rising Pleiades as a candidate for this temple. Select the cluster, catalogue entry M 45, from the shortlist. First model its seasons of visibility, then extract the Julian date of heliacal rising.

phases <- star.phases("M 45", year, hor)
plot(phases)

events <- phases$metadata$events
hr_date <- events$date.julian[events$event == "heliacal rising"]
hr_date
#> [1] "-3249/04/26"

Use the heliacal rising date from star.phases() in riseset() to find the instant the centre crosses the local skyline. Allow five minutes for it to climb into the opening. The phases use the cluster’s combined magnitude. The natural sketch represents unresolved cluster light as a faint patch. As its member stars become distinguishable they appear at their own positions and the patch fades.

rise <- riseset(hor, date = hr_date, obj = "M 45", calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")$rise
rise
#>          date     time  azimuth declination
#> 1 -3249/04/26 04:02:52 94.92541   -1.803325
rise_jd <- time2jd(paste(rise$date, rise$time), timezone = "UTC", calendar = "J")
sky <- sketchSky(hor, jd = rise_jd + 5 / 1440)
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = c(85, 105))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

On 26 April 3250 BCE the cluster centre rises at 04:02:52 UT. Five minutes later the group is about 1° above the ridge, near the doorway centre. None of the five brightest members clears the display threshold during the following 45 minutes. The phase calculation treats the cluster’s combined light (magnitude 1.60) as a single source. Its predicted date is therefore an estimate for the group. How readily the eye can pick out the cluster against dawn twilight also depends on how that light is spread across the sky. To compare like with like, repeat it for Alcyone, magnitude 2.87. Its heliacal rising falls on 2 May. At 1° above the ridge Alcyone is still too faint in the sketch. Sixteen minutes after rising it appears at 97.31°, 3.4° above the ridge and inside the doorway.

alcyone <- star.phases("Alcyone", year, hor)
events <- alcyone$metadata$events
date <- events$date.julian[events$event == "heliacal rising"]
alcyone_rise <- riseset(hor, date = date, obj = "Alcyone", calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")$rise
jd <- time2jd(paste(alcyone_rise$date, alcyone_rise$time), timezone = "UTC", calendar = "J")
sky <- sketchSky(hor, jd = jd + 16 / 1440)
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = c(85, 105), yrange = c(0, 11))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

Watch the cluster rise later in the season

Return 60 days after heliacal rising and calculate the new rising time. The two solid lines mark the edges of the doorway.

later <- riseset(hor, jd = rise_jd + 60, obj = "M 45", calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")$rise
sky <- sketchSky(hor, time = paste(later$date, later$time), calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")
sector <- c(85, 105)
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = sector, yrange = c(0, 11))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

Advance the same view by ten minutes to see the individual members clearing the ridge.

sky <- skyStep(hor, sky, minutes = 10, xrange = sector, yrange = c(0, 11))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

3. Multiple site analysis

Start with the Ġgantija entrance bearings

Select the Ġgantija phase in both datasets with the same row indices. First look at the central directions on a compass with plotAzimuth(). Then compare the full width of each opening with plotBars(). The bar extends from the measured left edge to the right edge.

rows <- which(malta_entrances$Phase == "Ggantija")
sites <- malta_entrances[rows, ]
profiles <- malta_horizons[rows]
width <- (sites[["Az R"]] - sites[["Az L"]]) %% 360
centre <- (sites[["Az L"]] + width / 2) %% 360
plotAzimuth(centre)

plotBars(centre, unc = width / 2, names = sites$Name, unit = "Azimuth")

Give each opening a distribution

For the method and its treatment of uncertainty, see Silva (2020). Here each bearing inside an opening is equally likely. Its half-width sets the uncertainty of a uniform distribution. Convert each entrance across its own skyline, then sum the declination distributions.

bearings <- az.pdf(pdf = "uniform", az = centre, unc = width / 2, name = sites$Name)
#> Using uniform azimuth uncertainties.
decs <- coordtrans(bearings, profiles)
plot(spd(decs))

Test random orientations

Would random bearings at these same sites produce concentrations as strong as the observed ones? Keep the measured widths and site profiles in the test. The following call reproduces the 500 simulations used here.

set.seed(42)
fit <- randomTest(decs, az = bearings, hor = profiles, nsims = 500)

The result is bundled so that building the vignette does not repeat the simulation.

fit <- readRDS(system.file("extdata", "ggantija-random-500.rds", package = "skyscapeR"))
plot(fit, show.local = TRUE)

fit$metadata$local.pval
#>   type start   end     p.value
#> 1    + -49.4 -48.2 0.556886228
#> 2    + -33.7 -28.6 0.003992016

The global p-value is 0.004. The strongest local excess lies between −33.7° and −28.6° declination (local p = 0.004).

Exploring celestial candidates

That peak warrants looking for possible celestial explanations. Search the locally significant interval across the dates of the Ġgantija phase.

bands <- fit$metadata$local.pval

#> 4           Mimosa    bet Cru  1.25  -33.56  -32.39

Several stars fit. For the archaeological and astronomical arguments for investigating Gacrux, see Silva and Lomsdalen (2025). Overlay its declination range on the same test.

gacrux <- skyObject("Gacrux", year = period)
plot(fit, obj = gacrux, show.local = TRUE)

Take Skorba West, one entrance in that band. Use its own horizon to find Gacrux’s heliacal rising date and crossing time. The vertical lines mark the two sides of this entrance.

i <- which(sites$Name == "Skorba West")
site_hor <- profiles[[i]]
opening <- c(sites[["Az L"]][i], sites[["Az R"]][i])
g_phases <- star.phases("Gacrux", year, site_hor)
g_events <- g_phases$metadata$events
g_date <- g_events$date.julian[g_events$event == "heliacal rising"]
g_rise <- riseset(site_hor, date = g_date, obj = "Gacrux",
                  calendar = "J", timezone = "UTC")$rise
g_rise
#>          date     time  azimuth declination
#> 1 -3249/10/12 03:52:50 129.9317    -30.6152
g_jd <- time2jd(paste(g_rise$date, g_rise$time), timezone = "UTC", calendar = "J")
sky <- sketchSky(site_hor, jd = g_jd + 20 / 1440)
plot(site_hor, sky = sky, xrange = c(122, 144), yrange = c(-1, 10))
abline(v = c(opening[1], mean(opening), opening[2]), col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

Gacrux crosses the ridge at 129.93° at 03:52:50 UT on 12 October 3250 BCE. The sketch shows it twenty minutes later at 132.99°, roughly 2.4° above the ridge and still inside the 128.55°–138.07° opening.

To test a particular explanation, modelTest() accepts an expected distribution, including a separate one for each site. Silva and Lomsdalen compared the orientations with expectations based on slope aspect, shelter from prevailing winds and exposure to winter sunlight. Each hypothesis gives a different set of expected directions against which to judge the observed concentration.

4. Other tools

Compare the three sky styles

Look east from Mnajdra South on the evening of 21 December 2026. Keep the time, horizon and field of view fixed. In the natural view, look for the stars you can still pick out close to the skyline. The two publication styles show the stellar pattern on a white background. Colour retains the stellar tints, while monochrome is suitable for a black-and-white figure.

sector <- c(0, 180)
heights <- c(-5, 85)
when <- "2026/12/21 20:00"

natural <- sketchSky(hor, time = when, timezone = "UTC", style = "natural")
plot(hor, sky = natural, xrange = sector, yrange = heights)

colour <- sketchSky(hor, time = when, timezone = "UTC", style = "publication-colour")
plot(hor, sky = colour, xrange = sector, yrange = heights)

mono <- sketchSky(hor, time = when, timezone = "UTC", style = "publication-mono")
plot(hor, sky = mono, xrange = sector, yrange = heights)

Compare constellation patterns

Use the publication colour style for both views. First draw the modern Western constellations over the eastern half of the sky.

sky <- sketchSky(hor, time = when, timezone = "UTC", style = "publication-colour",
                 display = list(constellations = "modern"))
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = sector, yrange = heights)
Modern Western constellations above the eastern horizon at Mnajdra South.

Now draw the asterisms of the 28 Chinese lunar mansions over the same stars. Look for the same bright stars in both panels and follow the different lines that connect them.

sky <- sketchSky(hor, time = when, timezone = "UTC", style = "publication-colour",
                 display = list(constellations = "chinese_mansions"))
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = sector, yrange = heights)
Chinese lunar-mansion asterisms visible in the same eastern sky.

To select just two modern figures, use constellations = c("Orion", "Taurus").

Select an individual figure with its stable identifier, such as "CHN009" for Horn. Use subset(constellations, set == "chinese_mansions") to browse the names and identifiers. See ?constellations for sources, licences, historical context and stellar-data limitations.

To draw your own constellation lines, import a Stellarium legacy constellationship.fab file. Each row contains a figure ID, the segment count, and two Hipparcos numbers per segment. This is the legacy text format, not Stellarium’s newer JSON sky-culture format. See the Stellarium User Guide, “Adding Sky Cultures” in older editions, for the line-file guidelines.

my_figures <- importConstellations("my-constellationship.fab")
# Subset rows here if only some figures should be drawn.
sky <- sketchSky(hor, time = "2026/12/21 20:00", timezone = "UTC",
                 display = list(constellations = my_figures))
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = sector)
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

The imported object stays in your script and can be saved with saveRDS(). It does not change the bundled constellation sets. skyStep() retains the custom selection. This imports line connections only; the endpoint stars must exist in the package’s stellar catalogue. It does not configure an external Stellarium installation. Credit the source and respect its sky-culture licence.

Explore the Milky Way and reference circles

The Milky Way appears with the stars by default. Its band follows diffuse stellar light, with atmospheric extinction dimming it towards the horizon. Use display = list(milky.way = FALSE) to hide it. Add galactic.equator = TRUE to the display list for its central reference circle, or ecliptic = TRUE and equator = TRUE for other reference paths. In a planisphere, the corresponding Milky Way switches are show.milky.way and show.galactic.equator.

Compare twilight in opposite directions

The natural sky changes with the Sun’s position. Compare the sunset direction with the opposite horizon at the same instant. Hiding the Moon isolates the solar contribution in this illustration.

dusk <- sketchSky(hor, time = "2025/02/01 16:40", timezone = "UTC",
                  style = "natural", display = list(moon = FALSE))
previous <- par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plot(hor, sky = dusk, xrange = c(211, 291), yrange = c(-3, 37))
plot(hor, sky = dusk, xrange = c(31, 111), yrange = c(-3, 37))
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

Capture a sky from Stellarium

For a rendered background, enable Remote Control in Stellarium Desktop and follow the screenshot setup in ?stellariumSky. A 4096-pixel capture is a starting point. Use a sketch when zooming beyond the available image detail.

sky <- stellariumSky(hor, time = "2026/12/21 20:00", timezone = "UTC",
                    width = 4096, display = list(atmosphere = TRUE, cardinals = FALSE))
plot(hor, sky = sky, xrange = sector)
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))
sky <- skyStep(hor, sky, minutes = -0.5, xrange = sector)
abline(v = door_frame, col = "firebrick", lty = c(1, 2, 1))

With either sky, assign each step to continue from the displayed time and repeat xrange to retain the sector. A sketch keeps its constellation selection. Stellarium takes a new screenshot. Redraw the doorway edges and centre after each step. Record the calendar and time zone alongside an archaeological reconstruction.

Equinoctial full Moons and solstitial crescent crossovers

Silva and Pimenta (2012) explore how the rising and setting positions of the Sun and Moon cross over. Two functions let us explore these events.

Which near-full Moon first meets the spring crossover condition? Inspect the paired lunar and solar observations returned by EFM().

full_moon <- EFM(year = 2026, loc = c(40, 0, 400), season = "spring", timezone = "UTC")
full_moon$Moon
#>   year       date     time  azimuth declination
#> 1 2026 2026/04/01 18:05:53 97.56414   -6.149152
full_moon$Sun
#>   year       date     time  azimuth declination
#> 1 2026 2026/04/01 05:45:39 83.58084    4.553975

For the winter first-crescent crossover, compare the setting positions and inspect the visibility diagnostics.

crescent <- CC(year = 2026, loc = c(40, 0, 400), season = "winter", crescent = "first",
               timezone = "UTC", simulation = "SIM1")
crescent$Moon
#>   year       date     time  azimuth declination      jd
#> 1 2026 2027/01/09 18:26:47 244.9829   -18.67155 2461415
crescent$diagnostics[, c("status", "visibility.margin", "day.offset")]
#>   status visibility.margin day.offset
#> 1     ok          9.339496          0

A planisphere for the site’s latitude

Place the bright stellar candidates in the wider sky for the same epoch. The Galactic equator provides a reference for the orientation of the Milky Way.

planisphere(year, lat = door$Latitude, max.mag = 3, show.galactic.equator = TRUE)

Add the Chinese lunar-mansion asterisms to the same chart. The lines connect catalogue stars even when an endpoint is fainter than the magnitude limit; only the part within the site’s accessible sky is drawn. This shows their arrangement across the celestial sphere, rather than their visibility at a particular time of night.

planisphere(year, lat = door$Latitude, max.mag = 3,
            show.galactic.equator = TRUE, constellations = "chinese_mansions")
